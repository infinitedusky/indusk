# Generation ship food system — outline drilldown

Working outline for a hard science-fiction book, built by the write skill's get-off-the-ground formula and then the outline drilldown: piece, arc, parts, each part roughly, sentences. Every entry is a purpose, never prose. Prose comes last, into the slots. Levels below the current one are empty on purpose.

Reset 2026-09-17: the first draft of this outline was a short story with a Snowpiercer-shaped allegory (elite parties while the ship fails, a revolt implied). Dropped. No revolt, one man, a slow failure.

## 0. Get off the ground

### The seed (user's words, lightly joined)

A generation ship that follows a chef from point A to point B: from learning he wants to be a chef, or being assigned it and knowing that is his future, all the way to running the show. He comes up with something very valuable. The realization the book leaves you with is that it only buys an extra generation: we need ten, we could make six, now we can make seven. Has my life's work really done anything? Managerially suspenseful, the way The Martian made the math suspenseful. The expected thing, the ship breaking down into tribes, does not happen; you would also expect a society not to accept this much separation of wealth, and historically it has not, but that no longer seems to be the case, and the book does not know why.

Added later in the same session: it is a coming-of-age story about a person whose experience and growth go past anything he dreamed of. A janitor's son from the lowest caste who happened to be very good at one thing. The other realization, beside the ledger: language and vocabulary are weaponized by elites, dressed up by themselves, internalized and believed as intelligence. Part of the financial system is genuinely on the tail of the curve; a huge part sits squarely in the middle, run and mythologized by people who think themselves so much smarter than the housekeeper or the farmer.

Added later still: the ship launched with a known gap, the way climate projections that stay under catastrophe assume direct air capture at scale by 2050. Part of the population has to sleep for the loop to close. The cryo tech was known and shipped working; a problem was noticed very early in the voyage; the ship asked Earth for help and was told it would be fixed. First "we need more time," then silence (a war, or nothing; Children of Time's silence). Without cryo the easy solution is to cull the population. His contribution: chasing the modernist-cookbook burger (sous vide, liquid-nitrogen dip, flash fry) on cultured meat, he sees what is wrong with the pods. And it is not enough, and the book says why.

### Purpose

- **Why**: to work on it with a good friend, a former catering chef who fed very large venues and left partly because the scale got to him (the fifteen cows behind one wedding). Let him solve the food problems. Do it together, for the two of us. And a science-fiction book with a chef at the centre is fun in itself.
- **Success**: not yet stated by the user. Proposed: a finished draft both names would go on.

### Ten books, and what each gives

1. **Seveneves** (Stephenson). A simple problem that cascades into others, and the reader pulled through by the cascade. Gives: the shape of the first failure. One number that does not close leads to the next.
2. **The Expanse novellas** (Corey). Short span, one point of view, one inflection. The span does not fit a life, so what it gives is form: each stage of the life could be a novella, days at an inflection point, not years.
3. **The Man Who Sold the Stars** (Benford, 2013, in Starship Century; confidence moderate on the attribution). A life from boy to old man, ending on the irony that the world moved past his life's work. Gives: the closest cousin to this book's verdict, and the warning that the verdict must not be nihilism.
4. **Snowpiercer**. Food as the visible form of class; the bugs. Gives: the specific that the allegory lives in, and the counter-example: what this book refuses (revolt).
5. **Aurora** (Kim Stanley Robinson). A generation ship as a closed system that cannot work, argued with numbers; the ship's AI as narrator, and Devi training it past the halting problem. Gives: hard-SF credibility for the loop's failure, and the difference this book has to earn: Aurora is about the ship, this is about a person inside the count.
6. **Snow Crash** and 7. **Cryptonomicon** (Stephenson). The technical digression as pleasure; the reader enjoys being taught a system. Gives: permission for the ledger and the loop to be entertaining on the page, and the reader who wants them.
8. **Children of Time** (Tchaikovsky). A generation ship breaking down, tribes forming, and a point of view that wakes across centuries. Gives: the deep-time structure, and the second counter-example: the breakdown this book says does not happen.
9. **The Martian** (Weir). Arithmetic as suspense, in a unit fixed on page one. Gives: the unit. Whatever the chef counts in has to be established early and never change.
11. **The Remains of the Day** (Ishiguro, 1989; the 1993 film has Hopkins as Stevens). A servant operating at the highest possible level, who understands exactly whom he served and what the house was used for, and whose understanding leads not to revolt but to a lament for the life he did not have. His word for the blindness is dignity, and he believes it. Gives: the shape of a realization that does not become revolt, and the protagonist's own vocabulary as the thing that lets him keep serving.
12. **Flowers for Algernon** (Keyes). The prose grows with the man, and it works because the progress reports are his: the growth is inside the story. Contrast Oxen of the Sun in Ulysses, where the style changes over the reader's head. Gives: register that grows with the protagonist, and the rule that it must be diegetic. The novella form supplies the frame: each novella is a different age of the same man.
10. **The Windup Girl** (Bacigalupi). A warmed world where the calorie is currency and the unit is the joule: a megodont winds a kink-spring, the spring stores joules, the joules came from a calorie company's seed. Gives: the unit, and the proof that a physics unit reads when it buys things.

### The round

Objections, each armed by a book, each labelled **open** (the user decides) or **answered** (where the answer lives).

1. From The Martian and The Windup Girl: **answered**, in the piece. The unit is the joule. Reactor to light to crop to plate to a day of a person's work is one ledger, and the chef and the engineer count in the same unit, which is what puts them in one room. A person needs about ten megajoules a day, so the reader can multiply by the headcount on page one. Open inside it: what a joule buys on the ship (a ration, a seat at a table, a lamp-hour over a crop bed). A joule that buys nothing is The Martian's calorie with a worse name.
2. From Seveneves: **answered**, in the arc. The first problem is dust. A closed ship is closed to mass, not energy (it must radiate heat; that is the design, not the leak), so what cannot fully close is mass, and dust is what trace mass loss looks like: skin, fibre, crop residue, machine wear. It is the janitor's son's inheritance; his father cleaned it, so he has always known that a system not supposed to make any makes it constantly. Nobody hid it; someone of his caste removed it, which is how a loss term becomes invisible. The smallest number on the ship, and the first that does not close. The question is not where it goes (it is recycled) but where it comes from and what it is made of. The cascade: dust on a ship with no soil and no wind is fibre and skin, mostly fibre. The design allowed closed-loop synthetic cloth plus a small natural-fibre allowance, a number in the school textbook. The fibre fraction he measures is several times the allowance, and under a scope it is wool: keratin with a scale pattern no human hair has. Wool means sheep, living animals on a ship where meat is cultured and every animal is a person's ration walking around. The design allowed a small flock, a genetic reserve; it did not allow a herd. He learns why there is a herd the way a chef would: lamb on the top table, while the ship eats the cultured steak he was proud of. The entitlement is real meat, and the wool is what real meat looks like in a filter. The math accounted for the sheep and forbade the behaviour; the behaviour was never hidden, it was cleaned. This plants the steak from the first novella as a gun. Held in reserve for later cascades: filter waste stored rather than returned, phosphorus, and which deck does not return its dead. Rejected alternative: heat lost to space as the deflationary leak; physically off, and the "numbers don't add up" trope.
3. From the Expanse novellas: **answered**, in the piece. One novella, not long, one inflection cut from a larger life: the days when the pods are proven to work and nobody uses them. Everything earlier (the steak, the dust, the schedule from school) is memory a working man would have; everything later is the book written afterwards if this one lands. Told from inside the days, not through a journal (considered, disliked). One span, one point of view, chosen once: the form axis conforms. Prose that grows with him is out; a single span cannot carry it, and dropping it removes the last form risk. The alternative held for the zoom-out: the teacher, told from the end the way Remains of the Day is, more literary, less suspenseful, and without the burger.
4. From The Man Who Sold the Stars: **answered**, in the arc. "Six, now seven, we need ten" would be a verdict the reader could write from page one if the finding were the number. It is not. The finding is the sight: a man who sees the entire clock, every gear, and is left impotent before a situation that defies solution, having worked toward solutions his whole life. The reader could not write that from page one because the gears are what the book spent five novellas teaching. The shrug survives only if the seeing cost him nothing; the close has to be the man who paid for the sight looking through it.
5. From Aurora: **answered**, in the piece. Aurora already proves the ship cannot work; a reader who has read it knows the loop fails. This book is not about whether the loop fails. It is about a person whose whole competence is inside the count.
6. From Snowpiercer and Children of Time: **declared**, not an axis deviation. The breakdown the genre expects does not happen, and the book does not explain why. Objection inside that: a book that explains the absence of revolt becomes a book about propaganda. The premise stays silent. Open whether the chef ever notices it.
7. From Stephenson: **answered**, in the reader line. The reader who enjoys four pages on where the nitrogen goes is the reader named below.
9. From the seed: **answered**, in the arc. What is the mental model of someone whose decisions cost a generation? Not malice; the book is dead if it is. Three parts, each with a real-world version. A discount rate: the future is priced lower at the going rate, and a generation away is worth about forty percent of now at three percent a year; they choose at the rate everyone uses. A unit that does not count people: they see joules, and only the chef converts joules into generations, so a table costs forty million joules, a number, not a person. Belief in the fix: someone will invent something. And he does. His invention buys one generation and is heard as proof the model was right. His best work is their best evidence.
   Note, from the effective-altruist turn to pricing future generations: the ship has both halves. An engineer argues the zero discount rate with a straight face and the chef's own numbers (Ramsey, 1928: discounting the future is ethically indefensible and comes from weakness of imagination). And the table adopts the vocabulary: every private lamp-hour is "an investment in the ship's future," said sincerely, at three percent. The language of pricing future generations is spoken most fluently by the people discounting them. Keep it to a phrase they use and one engineer who means it; a named movement four generations from Earth is a costume.
10. From the seed and The Remains of the Day: **answered**, in the arc. The vocabulary realization is the revolt book by other means if he discovers the elite are frauds; the reader sneers along and learns nothing. Two things keep it honest. Some of the engineers really are on the tail of the curve, and he respects them. And the realization includes himself: as lead he speaks the language fluently, catches himself being believed for it, and knows his steak was a luxury good too. He discovers that fluency is a currency, that he holds some, and that it converts to nothing in joules. His own word for what he does (craft, service, the standard; to be chosen) is his dignity in Stevens's sense: the word that lets him keep cooking. Understanding produces lament, not revolt: the life the kitchen took.
8. Carried over, **open**: why a chef holds the power to manage a life-support loop. Either the kitchen is where the loop is actually run, because what people will eat is the loop's largest variable and the engineers know it; or he has no power and can only cook. The seed says "running the show," which leans to the first.

### What is in and what is out

The spine, seven verbs; everything attaches to one or goes: **a janitor's son learns to cook, learns the loop, learns the ledger, learns the language, invents the thing, does the conversion, and teaches.**

In:

- The closed loop and the joule (learns the loop). The unit is fixed in the first novella and never changes.
- The steak (learns to cook). The opening, and the thing that turns out to have been a luxury good.
- Money seen from the kitchen (learns the ledger). One rule: it appears only where it touches his job.
- Fluency, and catching himself believed for it (learns the language). The coming-of-age interior and the Ishiguro cost.
- The table's mental model: discount rate, a unit that does not count people, belief in the fix (does the conversion). The allegory's content.
- The invention, six to seven against ten (invents the thing). The named deviation.
- Density in the room, economy on the spine. The spine keeps Chekhov's rule: the steak, the unit, the invention pay off because the reader was promised they would. The room may be as dense as Gravity's Rainbow and need not wrap up: overheard arguments, the currency, the table's talk. A gun is a promise; an overheard conversation is weather. Keep the guns few and the weather plentiful, so the reader knows which is which.
- No revolt, unexplained. The silent premise.

Out or demoted:

- The argument over the value of future generations (the effective-altruist turn): in, as weather. Overheard while serving, early, from his perspective, over his head and not the reader's, and never resolved. It is not on the spine and owes no payoff; the world does not resolve for a servant.
- Demurrage, Dojima, futures: texture for the ledger novella, material for the chef. Not on the arc.
- The financial system's tail versus its middle: absorbed into learns the language. As its own theme it is the sneer.
- Wealth as class in general: gone since the reset. The allegory stays the food system.
- Prose that grows with him (Flowers for Algernon): out of this novella, a single span cannot carry it. Held for the zoom-out, where the life is told whole.
- The whole life, school to teacher: the larger story this novella is cut from. Not this book.

### Named deviation (proposed)

The earned-verdict axis. The protagonist does the best work of anyone on the ship and it is not enough, and he will not live to see whether it mattered. Declared, so the reader can forgive it, and the reason objection 4 has to be answered well.

## 1. The piece

A hard science-fiction novella, not long, about a closed food system on an interstellar generation ship: the days when the ship's culinary director, chasing a perfect crust on cultured meat, makes the cryopods work, and nobody uses them. It is one inflection cut from a larger life (a janitor's son from the lowest deck, very good at one thing, grown past anything he dreamed of and past what he is comfortable with); that life is the book written afterwards if this one lands. The earlier life is present as memory. He wants to cook good food. He has to cook a staggering amount of it. He can see the loop cannot hold. The thing he invents buys one generation; they need four more.

The design gap: the loop closes only if part of the population sleeps. The pods shipped working; a flaw was found early in the voyage; the ship asked Earth, and Earth said it would be fixed, then that it needed more time, then nothing. Everyone aboard learned the fix in school as a schedule. Round-trip latency shapes the sequence: at a tenth of light speed a question sent at year ten is answered at year twelve, one sent at year thirty at year thirty-seven, so each exchange is longer than the last and by the end nobody can tell delay from silence. The conversation stops before Earth does. This is the allegory's sharpest instrument: every path that stays under catastrophe assumes a technology that does not exist yet.

The loop and the ledger are the same thing, seen from the kitchen. The ship's money is the loop's accounting made visible, and it appears only where it touches his job: what a plate costs, who can pay for the table he sets, what a silo of rot does to its owner versus the loop that gets it back, a menu promised against a harvest that has not happened. He never explains the system; the reader assembles it from what he has to pay attention to.

His arc is sophistication: from a cultured-meat steak that impresses a teacher and a classroom, to the conversion nobody else on the ship performs, joules into generations, and what it shows him about the people he cooks for.

**Allegory**, kept narrow: our food system in a warming world. Not wealth in general, not class in general. The person who can see the failure, whose lifetime it will not end in, and who decides what to cook now and what to teach.

**Reader** (proposed, unconfirmed): a hard-SF reader who will check the numbers, has read Aurora or Seveneves, and enjoys being taught a system.

**Unit**: the joule. Ten megajoules per person per day; the ship's headcount times that is the number the book is about.

**Thesis, one line** (proposed): the loop will not fail on his watch, and everything about whether it fails is decided on his watch.

## 2. The arc

What the reader holds at the start: a boy who wants to cook, a ship that works, a kitchen as a place of craft, and a cultured-meat steak good enough to impress a room.

What the reader holds at the end: an old man who sees every gear of the clock and can turn none of them, and knows the gear he is looking at is his own kitchen, a ledger the reader can now read in both units, joules and generations, a number that went from six to seven against a ten, the knowledge that the people he cooked for were accountants using his own unit and his invention was their evidence, and the question the book leaves: has his life's work done anything.

The engine, so the horizon has pressure in the present: in a closed loop, palatability is a loss term. Food nobody finishes is nutrient leaving the cycle. So cooking well is maintenance of the system, not decoration on it, until the day cooking well means inputs the loop cannot return. His three jobs (cook well, cook at scale, keep the loop alive) are one job, and the book is about the day they split.

Beats of the larger life, kept as the memory the novella draws on and as the map for the zoom-out. The novella itself is beat 4, with beat 5's end state and closing image; beats 1 to 3 are what he remembers:

1. School. The steak: cultured meat, made well enough to impress a teacher and a classroom. He learns to cook and learns the loop at the same time, without yet seeing they are the same subject. The reader gets the system's real mechanics here, the unit, the first sight of what a joule buys, and the schedule: the pods, the flaw (women who sleep wake infertile), the fix that Earth will send. Taught as a date, not a hope.
2. Apprentice. Dust, where there should be none. He sees the first number that does not close because he is the only one in the kitchen who ever cleaned. Someone older already knows and has chosen not to say. He learns what the kitchen does with a fact like that.
3. Lead. Earth has gone silent, or the silence is now old enough to be admitted. The staggering scale. He is now the one making the tradeoff every day: the good meal and the ledger. His art is both leak and patch, and the top tier's table is a line item he signs. He is fluent now in the table's language, and is believed for it. The life the kitchen is taking is visible to the reader before it is visible to him.
4. The invention. Chasing the perfect crust on cultured meat (sous vide, liquid-nitrogen dip, flash fry), he is doing cryobiology with a thermometer: ice crystals rupturing cells, the race to vitrify, cryoprotectants that are themselves toxic. Freezing meat without wrecking it and freezing tissue without killing it are one problem, and he sees the pods' flaw the way a chef would. The reader believes, with him, that it closes the gap. The arithmetic says one generation: the pods were meant to work from launch, the shortfall they were to cover has compounded for four generations, and you cannot un-eat. Working cryo stops the bleeding and restores nothing. Six becomes seven; ten does not move. And the people he cooks for hear it as proof that the fix always comes. This is where he performs the conversion, joules into generations, and sees their model from inside. The cull is silent and never agreed. Without cryo the design needs the population down near ten; about five hundred people are on life extension and will not die; so the rates drift. A ration formula rounded down, a birth licence deferred, medical priority to the people whose years the ledger values. Each defensible, none a policy, nobody decided, the way nobody decided to warm a planet. Everyone suspects; nobody can point to it. The kitchen is the instrument: the staggering amount he cooks is the number, and the number for the lower decks sits below maintenance by a formula nobody wrote in one sitting. Living to a hundred and forty is one less generation; that is the X. **Answered**, by the flaw itself: the pods stay empty. The flaw is that a woman who sleeps wakes infertile (the egg is the hardest human cell to freeze: large, water-heavy, a meiotic spindle that ice shatters; noticed early because the first sleepers' cohort had no children). So the flaw only ever cost the fertile. The old, the post-reproductive, the five hundred on life extension, could have slept from year ten with nothing to lose, and did not. The flaw was never the reason the pods stood empty; it was the story. His fix removes the story, and they still do not sleep, and now everyone can see it. Nothing is decided, which is this ship's only way of deciding. The invention works, is never used, and the ship does not collect even the one generation it bought. The shape of his insight, from the burger: sous vide then liquid nitrogen makes a surface that vitrifies and a core that does not, and one protocol applied to a non-uniform thing fails at one layer; the body was being treated as one tissue. Mechanism left to the chef and a cryobiologist.
5. Teacher. What he chooses to teach the next set: the craft, the ledger, or the number. The decision is shown, not stated. Understanding has produced no revolt; what it produced is the lament, and the reader should be able to name the life he did not have. End state: he sees the whole clock and can do nothing about it. Closing image, a meditation: the reflection of stars on a window. The ship spins, so it is not a view out; it is a representation, and he knows exactly how it is made, like the ledger, like the vocabulary, and it is beautiful anyway. A man who can see through everything, choosing to look at it.

Open questions before the parts level:

- The voyage math. How far along is the ship, how many generations to arrival, and is "ten" arrival or something else? This is the book's arithmetic and belongs in beat 1.
- What the top tier actually gets that the loop cannot return. Animal protein? Fresh over processed? Variety?
- What the invention is. It has to be a chef's invention, not an engineer's, or objection 8 reopens.
- Title.

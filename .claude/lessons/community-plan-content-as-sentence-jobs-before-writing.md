# Plan short-form writing as numbered sentence jobs before any wording exists

For anything short that will be sent or submitted in the user's name (an application box, a cover note, an outreach message), do not start with a draft and do not edit sentences in place. Work in this order:

1. **State the reader's prompt and constraints first.** A direct question wants its answer in sentence one. An open box ("share anything you want us to know") needs a framing sentence, because the reader doesn't know what's coming. Judge no sentence until this is written down. The failure this prevents: applying a rule learned on one prompt shape to a different one.
2. **Write the arc as a numbered list of sentence jobs.** One line per sentence: what it does for the reader (frame, reason one flat, the seat, the stack that earns an observation, the verdict, the gate, the action, the tie to their posting, the close). The user owns the structure and reorders or splits.
3. **Refine with examples.** One candidate sentence under each job, drawn from the record or the user's own dictation. Where a job can be filled two ways, show both.
4. **Then the user writes freely.** The slots are fixed, so the language can be theirs. The agent repairs grammar and strips tells, and flags any move it would drop instead of dropping it.
5. **Lock sentences one at a time**, dated, in the working file. The final piece is assembled from the locked arc.

Why: a wrong sentence gets caught as "wrong job," which is arguable, instead of "sounds off," which isn't. And the wording stays the user's because they're filling slots, not reacting to the agent's prose. Learned 2026-09-16 after three rounds of sentence-level edits on an open-box application field went in circles, one of them on a critique that was wrong for the prompt shape. Long-form pieces already do this at paragraph grain via the `write` skill's outline rule; this is the same discipline one level down.
